Константин:
Problems it solves:

No doctor in space and no real-time communication with Earth. In an acute emergency (such as chest pain or fainting halfway to Mars or inside a lunar crater), a crew of non-physicians is left alone to deal with a critical condition.

Hidden fatigue and sensor lag. The CO₂ gas sensor inside a spacesuit lags by 5–10 minutes, and astronauts often fail to notice—or deliberately hide—overexertion, increasing the risk of not making it back to base before running out of oxygen.

Gradual physiological deconditioning. In microgravity and low gravity (0g / 1/6g), bone density drops rapidly, muscles atrophy, and the cardiovascular system, immunity, and mental health suffer.

How it solves them (in brief):

Prevents crises during spacewalks (Mode 1 · EVA): tracks leg muscle fatigue and oxygen consumption while accounting for actual crater slope, calculates the "point of no return" countdown (Walk-Back Limit) in advance, and displays a short command on the visor and wrist display.

Guides a non-physician step-by-step in an emergency (Mode 2 · Emergency): automatically activates emergency triage when an alarm is triggered or a fall occurs—identifies the diagnosis using 4 visual symptom cards and voice, strictly blocks dangerous mistakes (such as prohibiting nitroglycerin when blood pressure is low, BP < 90), reads out step-by-step first aid instructions, and queues a report for Flight Surgeons on Earth.

Catches hidden exhaustion in the evening (Mode 3 · Evening): compares the astronaut's self-assessment against actual biometrics (Mismatch detector), assigns recovery quests, monitors 6 body systems via a clickable body figure, and prepares a summary for the commander to adjust the schedule.

Slide 5: How It Works and What Data It Is Trained On
How the system works (3 simple steps):

Collecting data from the astronaut: sensors in the base layer garment and spacesuit read heart rate, blood pressure, breathing, muscle oxygen, and gait stability every second.
Smart onboard analysis (without a connection to Earth): the base's onboard computer runs this data through our algorithms and NASA standards:
Fatigue and oxygen forecast: warns in advance how many minutes until the legs get tired and calculates how much time is left before the "point of no return" so the astronaut is guaranteed to make it back to the base.
Emergency assistant: if a medical episode or a fall occurs, the system automatically identifies the cause, prevents dangerous mistakes (for example, it won't allow administering a medication that causes a sharp drop in blood pressure), and dictates simple rescue steps out loud to the crew.
Evening check-in: compares how the astronaut feels subjectively with what the instruments show—if they are hiding fatigue, the system will notice and suggest the commander adjust the EVA schedule.
Clear screen and visor: everything is displayed on the base console as a traffic light (green / yellow / red), a clickable body map, and short prompts directly on the helmet visor and wristwatch.
What real data it is trained and calibrated on:

Lunar terrain and NASA standards:
NASA LRO LOLA Moon maps (Site04) — real elevations and slopes of Shackleton Crater to calculate climbing exertion.
NASA Health Standard (Human Health and Performance) — official health norms, fatigue thresholds, and EVA clearance rules.
Apollo EVA archives and the Lunar EVA 2023 study — real benchmarks for heart rate and energy expenditure in lunar gravity (1/6 g).
Space biology (NASA OSDR):
Study OSD-783 — data on muscle weakening and atrophy without gravity.
Study OSD-435 — data on the effects of space radiation on the heart and blood vessels.
Medical databases (PhysioNet and PLOS ONE) for model training:
Muscle fatigue: PhysioNet sEMG and the PLOS ONE muscle endurance study — drop in oxygen and strength in the legs under load.
Energy expenditure and respiration: PhysioNet Treadmill and PhysioNet ACTES — how heart rate and oxygen consumption rise during uphill walking and intense work.
Emergency medicine: PhysioNet BIDMC and PhysioNet Pulse Transit Time — real clinical recordings of heart rate, blood pressure, respiration, and blood oxygen to recognize acute conditions.
